July 31, 1976 · Big Thompson Canyon, Colorado

Climb to Safety

Fifty years ago, a storm that refused to move turned a Colorado canyon into a raging river in a single night. What separated the people who survived from the ones who did not came down to one simple choice.

Part I

The Radio Call

SGT. WILLIS HUGH PURDY · BIG THOMPSON CANYON · ~9:15 P.M.
"I'm stuck. I'm right in the middle of it. I can't get out..."

Those were the last words anyone heard from Sgt. Willis Hugh Purdy. It was around 9:15 p.m. on July 31, 1976. He was in Big Thompson Canyon, and the river was rising fast around his patrol car. Before the water took him, he had warned dozens of people to get out and ordered evacuations lower in the canyon. That order is credited with saving hundreds of lives. He was never heard from again.

He was one of 144 people who died that night in what is still Colorado's deadliest natural disaster. Five people were never found. Researchers later found that surviving came down to one basic move. People had to get out of the water's path and go uphill. Lori Peek, director of the Natural Hazards Center at the University of Colorado Boulder, says the people who stayed in their vehicles faced the highest risk. This story is about that choice, and why it was so hard to make.

Part II

The Canyon

July 31 was the night before Colorado's 100th birthday, and thousands of people had come to Big Thompson Canyon for the centennial weekend. An estimated 3,500 people were camping, fishing, and relaxing in the canyon that night. U.S. Highway 34 runs right beside the Big Thompson River between steep canyon walls. According to Denver Post archives, the river was usually only about 18 inches deep.

Above them, a massive storm stalled against the mountains. Up to 7.5 inches of rain fell in about an hour, and about 12 inches fell in just a few hours, according to the U.S. Geological Survey. The rocky, steep slopes could not hold it. "It was almost like a bathtub effect," said Neil Grigg, a civil and environmental engineering professor at Colorado State University.

Part III

The Night

5:00 PM

Upper Canyon

Light rain started in the upper canyon around 5 p.m.

Light rain started in the upper canyon around 5 p.m. and turned heavy by 6:30. Around 8, people began calling 911 about rocks on the highway, and a Colorado State Patrol trooper went to investigate. By 8:45, the heaviest rain began to fall, dumping as much as a year's worth of rain in an hour or two in many areas.

At 9 p.m., forecasters in Denver issued a bulletin warning of slow-moving thunderstorms that could cause local flooding. Fifteen minutes later, at 9:15, Purdy made his last radio call. Around 10:30, a wall of water nearly 20 feet tall tore down the canyon, and more than 400 cars were swept off the road that night. The National Weather Service issued its first flash-flood bulletin at 11 p.m., about thirty minutes after the main wave had already hit.

Part IV

The Ledge

Connie Granath Hays and her friend Terry Belair were driving in the canyon when the floodwaters began rising around them. They abandoned their car and climbed the canyon wall to reach higher ground.

They held on until daybreak, when a helicopter finally lifted them out. Fifty years later, Granath Hays told Denver7 that the two of them came to realize how fortunate they were. Their choice was the simplest one available: leave the car and go up.

Part V

The Morning After

When the sun came up on August 1, helicopters airlifted more than 800 survivors off ledges and rooftops, while at least 250 people were treated for injuries. In Loveland, refrigerated trucks were parked outside the hospital to serve as temporary morgue space. In the end, 144 people were dead, and U.S. Geological Survey records show the flood destroyed 418 homes and 52 businesses. Estes Park Officer Michael Conley also drowned while helping victims, along with Purdy. Five people were never found: Teresa Graham, 10, Evelyn Kindred, 67, George McCarty, 21, Vernon Oler, 51, and Rochelle Rogers, 2.

Research from Eve Gruntfest's 1977 study helped lead to the yellow warning signs now posted along Highway 34 and in canyons across Colorado. They read, "In case of flash flood, climb to safety." Peek says the sign has lasted because it tells people clearly what to do.

The sign does not ask anyone to read a rain gauge or judge the sky. It asks them to go up. Almost everything about that night was complicated. The one thing that was not was where to go.
